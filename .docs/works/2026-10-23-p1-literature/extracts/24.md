Bibliographic check: Yes. The title, the three authors (Allison J.B. Chaney, Brandon M. Stewart, Barbara E. Engelhardt), and the venue (RecSys 2018, Vancouver) all match. Small note: the arXiv ID comes from October 2017, but the 2018 year matches the RecSys version and the v2 header.

Says:
The paper shows that recommendation systems trained on data that they helped create can make users behave more alike. The authors test this with a simulation of 100 users, 1,000 time steps and six recommendation methods. The extra similarity does not bring more usefulness for users. Users with lower utility are hit harder by this effect. The authors suggest weighting methods and logging the recommender's behavior as partial fixes. The data are simulated, not real.

Answers:

Q1. Definition of algorithmic confounding.
Section 1, Figure 1 caption, page 1. The paper has no separate formal definition of the term. The closest definition is the Figure 1 caption: "Confounding occurs when a platform attempts to model user behavior without accounting for recommendations."

Q2. Simulation design and data.
Section 5.1, page 4. The community has 100 users and runs for 1,000 time intervals: "a community consists of 100 users and is run for 1,000 time intervals". Ten new items enter at each interval. The six algorithms are popularity, content filtering, matrix factorization, social filtering, random and ideal. Users and items are modelled with latent utility vectors (Section 4, Definition 1 and Assumption 2, page 3). In each step every user picks one item, and the choice depends on rank and known preference (Assumptions 5 and 6, page 3). There are two feedback settings: one training after 50 start-up iterations, and repeated training after 10 start-up iterations (Section 5.1, page 4). The data are synthetic. The real platforms MovieLens and Douban are only named as examples (Section 6, page 6).

Q3. Main findings on homogeneity and utility.
Section 5.2 (Figures 3 and 4, page 5), Section 5.3 (Figure 5, page 5 to 6), Section 5.4 (Figure 6, page 6). With repeated training, all non-random systems make users more alike than ideal utility needs: "systems that include repeated training homogenize user behavior more than is needed for ideal utility." Homogenization keeps rising with each loop cycle without utility gains. Popularity causes the most global homogenization (Figure 4). Users with lower utility have higher homogenization with their nearest neighbour (Figure 5). Matrix factorization and content filtering homogenize similarly, but matrix factorization gives a more unequal item consumption (Figure 6). The text gives no numbers for these effects. Values appear only on figure axes.

Q4. Limits of simulation results.
Section 5.3, page 5. The paper has no clear statement that simulations show what can happen but not what happens in real systems. The closest caveat is about fixed utility: "Note that we have assumed that each user/item pair has fixed utility". The authors add that real utility would probably make the effects larger (page 5). The conclusion (Section 7, page 7) limits the scope to simulated systems, in plain words.

Q5. Order, time structure, sampling bias.
Section 4, Assumption 5, page 3. Time is modelled in steps: "each user u will interact with exactly one item". Assumption 1 treats utility as static over time (page 3). Assumption 6 says each user interacts with an item at most once, and Assumption 7 says new and recommended items are interleaved (page 3). Rank position affects clicks (page 3). Section 3 (page 2) says users introduce selection bias, but the authors do not correct for it and aim to predict behaviour instead. Section 5.1 (page 4) trains each iteration on all previous data.

Not found:
- A standalone formal definition of algorithmic confounding (only the Figure 1 caption and the general text in Sections 2 and 3).
- A clear statement that simulation results show only what can happen in real systems.
- Numeric effect sizes in the text (no Jaccard or Gini values). Only figure axis tick labels are visible in the text.
- Any experiment on real user data.
- Any model of temporal dependence between a user's interactions beyond the assumptions listed in Q5.
