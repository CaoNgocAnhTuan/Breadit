Says:
The paper studies a common shortcut in recommender papers: ranking the relevant item against only a small random set of other items. It shows that this shortcut can change the results, so a model that is better on the full ranking can look worse on the sampled one, even on average. Only AUC stays consistent, and with small samples all metrics look alike. The authors advise avoiding sampling; if it is needed, a correction lowers bias but raises variance.

Answers:

Q1. Main claim. Section 1, p.1. Quote (part of one sentence): "sampled metrics (even in expectation) can be inconsistent"
In words: sampled metrics can disagree with the exact metrics, even on average over many repetitions.

Q2. Metrics studied: AUC, precision at k, recall at k, average precision (AP) and NDCG, defined in Section 3, p.2-3. Section 3.1 (p.3) says reciprocal rank, hit ratio and accuracy reduce to AP, recall and precision at 1. The experiments in Section 6 use Recall@10, NDCG@10, AP and AUC. Section 4.2 (p.4) finds only AUC consistent. In words: a sampled score can rank two models in the opposite order from the exact score, so it can name the wrong best model. Quote: "The other metrics are inconsistent."
Section 4.2, p.4.

Q3. Avoid or correct? Both, with avoiding first. Section 8, p.9. Quote: "sampling should be avoided as much as possible during evaluation."
In words: Section 7 (p.9) says that if sampling is needed, use a correction method and rerun with different seeds. The corrected metric has less bias but more variance, and only avoiding sampling removes the bias.

Q4. Datasets and models: binarized MovieLens 1M with a holdout-last-event split, as in reference [9] (Section 6, p.7). Quote: "the same dataset (binarized Movielens 1M [8])"
In words: 6040 test users (Section 6.1, p.7). Recommender X is matrix factorization (implicit ALS, d=16). Recommenders Y and Z are item-based collaborative filtering with different settings (Section 9, p.10). Sample sizes: m=100 in the main experiment (Section 6, p.7); m=99 in the toy example (Section 4.2, p.3); the toy example has n=10,000 items and 5 instances (Section 3.2, p.3); Figure 6 (p.8) varies m up to 1000.

Q5. Yes. Table 3 (Section 6.2, p.7-8): on exact Recall, NDCG and AP, X is the worst and Z the best, but on the sampled metrics X looks best. Table 2 and Figure 2 (Section 4.2, p.3-4) show the toy-example order of A, B and C changing for AP, NDCG and Recall as the sample size grows. Quote: "These results indicate that sampled metrics can be inconsistent"
The text-extracted tables are partly garbled, so I used only the order statements, not the numbers.

Q6. Full catalogue with a large catalogue: partly. Section 4, p.3. Quote: "Ranking all items is expensive when the number of items, n, is"
In words: the paper says full ranking is costly for large n, which is why sampling is used. It does not give a method, timing or cost numbers for full ranking on a large catalogue. Its own MovieLens experiment ranks all items for each test user (Section 6.1, p.7), but the item count of that data is not given. Section 1 (p.1) says industrial catalogues are often many millions of items.
